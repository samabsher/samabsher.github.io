01 · The Operation

A wall of agents

Before 1993, the Border Patrol mostly looked for unauthorized immigrants after they were already inside El Paso — a practice that amounted to stopping and searching people based on how they looked, and that ended in a federal class-action lawsuit. When the court granted relief, a new sector chief flipped the strategy: stop crossings from happening at all.

On September 19, 1993, Operation Hold the Line put a visible line of agents along the border — over 400 of the sector's 650 agents, positioned within sight of each other, around the clock, backed by four helicopter patrols and a repaired nine-mile downtown fence. It worked on its own terms: apprehensions around El Paso fell 80–85% within weeks. Local approval topped 90%, and the operation became the template for border militarization in California and beyond.

“The rampant criminal problems in our downtown are gone.” — Larry Francis, Mayor of El Paso, 1993

02 · Two Cities, One Economy

The line ran through a single city

El Paso and Ciudad Juárez are not neighbors so much as halves. In the early 1990s roughly 20% of all U.S.–Mexico overland trade moved through the area and over 150,000 people crossed the border every day — to work, shop, attend school, see a doctor. Juárez residents supplied labor to El Paso firms and an estimated 20–30% of the city's retail sales. Nearly two-thirds of El Pasoans spoke Spanish at home.

Cutting crossings in a place like that is not a surgical strike on illegal entry. It reduces the supply of workers, the demand for goods, and the customer base of downtown businesses — all at once. Whether the net effect on El Paso was positive or negative is an empirical question. So we measured it.

03 · The Measuring Stick

Compared to what?

You can't rerun the 1990s without the operation, so we build the counterfactual two independent ways.

Households. Using the 5% Census samples from IPUMS — 70,272 household records — we compare El Paso before (1990) and after (2000) the operation against three southwestern control cities: Tucson, Albuquerque, and Laredo, in a difference-in-differences design reweighted on age, sex, marital status, education, children, and Hispanic origin.

The county. With annual county data we build a “synthetic El Paso” — a weighted blend of Pima (Tucson), Bernalillo (Albuquerque), and Webb (Laredo) counties, with weights chosen so the blend tracks real El Paso for the fifteen-plus years before 1993. If the blend matches El Paso's path up to the operation, its path afterward is a credible estimate of the El Paso that never got one. Annual data lets us verify the pre-treatment fit directly — and it is excellent.

04 · Incomes and Homes

What households lost

By 2000, the average El Paso household earned $2,714 less than the control cities say it should have — a 6% drop, significant at the 0.1% level. House values fell further: $23,194 below a counterfactual average of roughly $100,000. Since a house price capitalizes everything a location offers — jobs, amenities, safety — that 23% decline is a rough hedonic estimate of the operation's net effect on living in El Paso. Buyers who could price in the safer downtown priced in everything else too, and marked the city down.

How do we know it isn't noise? The placebo test

Assign the “treatment” falsely to each control city and rerun the model. Nothing comes close to El Paso's decline:

In-space placebo “effects” (weighted DiD)
Falsely treated Income House value
El Paso (actual) −2,714*** −23,194***
Albuquerque +480 +4,084**
Tucson +559 +2,395
Laredo +1,002 −5,776**

Placebo income “effects” are small, positive, and insignificant. The only sizeable placebo on house values is a quarter the size of El Paso's, with mixed sign.

06 · The Bargain, Priced

Crime fell. It wasn't close to enough.

Crime reduction was the operation's selling point, so credit it in full: monetize every avoided property and violent crime using standard cost-of-crime values — generous ones. Then set those benefits against the income losses. The ledger is not close in any year:

Crime benefit combines the property- and violent-crime columns of the paper's Table 5. Summing the net column: roughly −$5.9 billion over nine years, borne by a county of about 700,000 people. These figures count only income and crime — they exclude the operation's direct budget cost, which would push the net further down.

The debate over border enforcement is usually a national one — about deportations, crime rates, and labor markets in the aggregate. What this ledger shows is a cost that debate mostly ignores: the bill handed to the border communities themselves.

07 · Kick the Tires

For researchers (and skeptics)

The full batteries are in the paper; the short versions are here.

Two designs, two datasets, one answer
The household evidence (Census microdata, difference-in-differences) and the county evidence (annual aggregates, synthetic control) share no data and few assumptions. The DiD cannot show pre-trends; the synthetic control can, and its pre-1993 fit is excellent. Both find economically large, statistically significant losses.
Maybe El Paso was just different? The placebo tests
Falsely treating Tucson, Albuquerque, or Laredo produces income “effects” of +$480 to +$1,002 — none significant, all the wrong sign for the skeptic's story. In the county analysis, El Paso's gaps exceed every constructible placebo in nearly all years.
Sensitive to the three-county donor pool?
No. Expanding the pool to 15 counties leaves the pre-treatment fit excellent and the post-1993 income decline negative and significant, beginning right at the intervention.
Isn't the house-value decline double counting?
We treat it as an alternative summary, not an addition. Because house prices capitalize local wages, amenities, and safety together, the ~23% relative decline is best read as a hedonic estimate of the operation's overall net effect — an independent check that lands in the same place as the income-based ledger.
